### SECTION 2 — INVESTMENT THESIS

**1. Dominant affordable-housing franchise with a widening moat.**
Home First is among the very few profitable, scaled pure-play affordable HFCs in India — ₹1,69,379 Mn AUM, 1,45,117 customers, 175 branches across 13 states/UTs, 25.7% YoY AUM growth and 34.5% YoY PAT growth in Q1 FY27 alone[^ip-latest]. The moat is built on four layers: (a) deep branch density in low-/mid-GDP states (Gujarat 28.6%, Maharashtra 15.2%, MP 10.7%, Tamil Nadu 10.4% of AUM) where bank competition is structurally weaker; (b) tech-led underwriting that approves 85% of loans within 48 hours — a customer-experience advantage in this segment; (c) a bureau-quality customer base (avg bureau score 749, 88% with prior credit history vs 74% three years ago); and (d) origination yield of 13.0% (Q1 FY27) that competitors in prime housing cannot match without abandoning their underwriting discipline[^ip-latest][^cc-transcript].

**2. Structural margin story is intact and expanding.**
Net Interest Margin improved to 6.0% in Q1 FY27 (from 5.9% in Q4 FY26); spread ex-co-lending holds at 5.3% (+20 bps YoY); and the cost-to-income ratio improved a sharp 330 bps YoY to 32.5% in FY26, the best in the affordable-HFC peer set[^ip-latest][^ar-fy25]. Cost of borrowing fell 10 bps QoQ to 7.8% in Q1 FY27 — and because the book is fully floating (except one NHB fixed-coupon tranche), rate-cut benefits flow through with minimal lag. Incremental spread is 5.40% vs 5.3% on the stock — meaning every fresh rupee of lending is more profitable than the legacy book, a textbook sign of a margin-accretive growth phase[^cc-transcript]. ROA of 4.2% (+50 bps YoY) is structurally supported and superior to most peers.

**3. Diversification into high-growth, policy-anchored adjacencies.**
Three adjacencies meaningfully de-risk the core model: (a) **Green Homes** — 550 IGBC-certified homes cumulatively (100 added in Q1), funded by a ₹280 Cr IFC line (7-year tenor) and $75 Mn DFC facility for women low-income borrowers — a differentiated ESG-led pool; (b) **PMAY 2.0** — 38,507 cumulative beneficiaries assisted with ₹966.4 Cr subsidy credit (≈26% loan reduction), positioning HOMEFIRST as a primary channel partner for the renewed PMAY scheme; and (c) **Co-Lending + Direct Assignment** — 12 co-lending and 8 DA partners including SBI, HDFC, ICICI, Bajaj Housing, Tata Capital, Bank of Baroda, with ₹3,313 Mn transacted in Q1 FY27 alone[^ip-latest][^ar-fy25]. These adjacencies are not just optics — they materially lower capital intensity (leverage 3.5x with CRAR 42.6%) and unlock fee income.

**4. Near-term catalysts (next 6–12 months).**
(a) Branch ramp — 61 new physical branches + 137 digital branches in pipeline; each ₹5–5.5 Cr/sales-employee productivity benchmark implies meaningful AUM tailwind[^cc-transcript]; (b) UP/UP-Uttarakhand acceleration — management explicitly identified UP as the highest medium-term (1–3 year) opportunity, with current AUM share just 6.2%; (c) sustained 4.5% BT-out (balance-transfer, i.e., customers refinancing away) — if maintained for 2–3 more quarters, it becomes a structural positive; (d) rating upgrade cycle — current AA from ICRA/CARE/India Ratings leaves headroom for an upgrade, which would lower incremental cost of funds; (e) potential ₹150 Cr NCD issue (board meeting held Aug 21, 2026) to further diversify the lender mix; and (f) CFO succession announcement — replacing Nutan Patwari (exits Aug 31, 2026) with a strong candidate would remove a key overhang[^cc-transcript].

**5. Biggest structural risk — concentration in self-employed & low-ticket customers in a slowdown.**
58% of AUM is EWS/LIG (economically weaker sections / low-income groups), 32% of customers are self-employed, and geographic concentration in Gujarat (28.6%) + Maharashtra (15.2%) leaves HOMEFIRST exposed to regional shocks — historical stress in Tirupur (Tamil Nadu textile belt) and Surat (diamond polishing) demonstrated that localized downturns can flow into 1+ DPD[^cc-transcript][^vp-thread]. GNPA at 1.8% looks benign, but 1+ DPD of 4.7% suggests underlying stress is real; further, the affordable-customer segment is structurally more vulnerable to monsoon shocks, informal-sector income disruptions, and any RBI tightening that raises EMI burdens in a fully floating book — the absence of meaningful fixed-rate hedging makes the P&L sensitive to rate hikes.[^ip-latest]

### SECTION 3 — BUSINESS OVERVIEW

**Core Business Model.**
Home First Finance Company India Ltd is a Mumbai-headquartered, RBI-registered Housing Finance Company (HFC) that lends primarily to first-time homebuyers in the affordable segment — Economically Weaker Sections (EWS), Low-Income Group (LIG) and lower-Middle Income Group (MIG) categories[^ar-fy25]. Think of it as a specialist bank that does one job extremely well: it gives home loans to salaried and self-employed customers who earn roughly ₹15,000–₹60,000 per month and want to buy a house worth ₹8–₹25 lakh. The company makes money on the **net interest spread** — the gap between what it charges borrowers (origination yield 13.0% in Q1 FY27) and what it pays lenders (cost of borrowing 7.8%)[^ip-latest]. That spread of ~5.3% on a ₹16,938 Cr book generates annualised interest income of roughly ₹1,750–₹1,800 Cr. A second, smaller revenue stream comes from **Direct Assignment (DA)** gains (₹364.8 Mn in Q1 FY27) — these are profits from selling chunks of loans to banks/HFCs that want book on their own balance sheet. A third stream is processing fees and other operating income.

**Revenue Split by Division (Q1 FY27, % of AUM).**
The asset book breaks down into three lending verticals:

*LTV = Loan-to-Value, the loan amount as a % of property value — a lower LTV means the lender has a bigger safety cushion if the borrower defaults.*

The revenue P&L is much simpler than the asset split suggests: of total income of ₹539.8 Cr in Q1 FY27, **interest on term loans contributes 81.4%** (₹439.3 Cr), DA gains contribute 6.8% (₹36.5 Cr), and "other income" (deposit interest, fees, commission, operating income) contributes 11.9% (₹64.1 Cr)[^ip-latest]. Net Interest Income is ₹231.5 Cr (42.9% of total income) and Net Total Income — NII + fees + DA gains — is ₹332.1 Cr (61.5% of total income). Operating expenses absorb 32.7% of NTI, credit cost is 40 bps of average assets, and the resulting PAT is ₹159.9 Cr (29.6% of NTI). For FY26 the full-year picture was total income ₹1,922.7 Cr and PAT ₹540.4 Cr (PAT margin 28.1%)[^ar-fy25].

**Key Customer Profile & Underwriting Edge.**
The customer is the under-banked Indian household — 1,45,117 active customers, 68% salaried (teachers, ITI workers, small-business employees) and 32% self-employed (kirana store owners, tailors, small traders). 12% of AUM is "new-to-credit" (i.e., these borrowers did not have a formal loan history when HOMEFIRST first lent to them); on the customer count, this is 17%, meaning HOMEFIRST is genuinely bringing first-time borrowers into the formal credit system — this is also why the company's mission aligns with RBI's financial-inclusion priorities[^ip-latest]. Average bureau score is 749 (above the prime 750 threshold), and 88% of customers have a prior credit history vs 74% three years ago — both metrics confirm portfolio quality has improved over time. **85% of loans are approved within 48 hours**, a meaningful speed-of-service moat in affordable housing where customers often need funds tied to a builder's payment schedule.

**Subsidiary Structure.**
HOMEFIRST operates with no material subsidiaries disclosed in the consolidated structure; the operating entity is the listed company itself. The capital structure benefits from a clean holding — there are no listed subsidiaries or complex JV arrangements on the balance sheet that need separate valuation[^ar-fy25]. The promoter footprint is small (9.34% as of Jun 2026) and dominated by private-equity sponsors True North Fund V LLP and Aether (Mauritius) Limited, which together hold the residual private-equity stake after multiple rounds of dilution.

**Manufacturing (Operational) Footprint.**
"Housing finance" doesn't have factories — but it has branches, and each branch is the equivalent of a manufacturing unit. HOMEFIRST runs **175 physical branches (+4 added in Q1 FY27) and a further 61 physical branches + 137 digital branches planned**, giving 373 touchpoints across 145 districts in 13 states/UTs[^ip-latest]. Headcount stood at 1,988 (up 133 QoQ), implying ~11 employees per branch — a deliberately tight staffing model that supports the cost-to-income ratio. Geographic mix is concentrated in western/central India: Gujarat 28.6%, Maharashtra 15.2%, Madhya Pradesh 10.7%, Tamil Nadu 10.4%, Telangana 7.9%, Rajasthan 6.7%, UP/Uttarakhand 6.2%, Karnataka 5.9%, Andhra Pradesh 5.4%[^ip-latest].

**Promoter Background & Group Context.**
This is the unusual part of the story: HOMEFIRST does **not** have a traditional Indian promoter family. It is a private-equity-built, professionally-run HFC. The two original sponsors — **True North (an Indian mid-market PE firm) and Aether (Mauritius)** — together hold only ~7% today, down from 30.19% in Sep 2023 as the company has gone through three rounds of dilution (IPO in 2019, follow-on raises, and a ₹1,250 Cr QIP in FY26)[^ar-fy25]. The Board has 5 of 7 directors as Independent (71%), with the Chairman being Independent — a strong governance signal for an HFC. Promoter pledging is zero. The MD & CEO is Manoj Viswanathan, who has been in the seat since the company's early years and has overseen the journey from a sub-₹1,000 Cr AUM start-up to the current ₹16,938 Cr franchise[^cc-transcript][^ar-fy25]. This is a professional-management, sponsor-oversight model — closer to a Bajaj Finance DNA than to the typical family-promoted HFC.

### SECTION 4 — INDUSTRY & COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE

**Total Addressable Market (TAM).**
India's affordable-housing finance market is structurally one of the largest under-penetrated credit pools in the world. The overall Indian housing finance market is estimated at ~₹30 lakh crore (USD 360 billion at ~₹83/USD) as of FY26, of which the affordable segment (loans up to ₹25 lakh to EWS/LIG) accounts for roughly ₹8–10 lakh crore (USD 100–120 billion)[^vp-thread]. The affordable-HFC TAM is projected to grow at a 18–22% CAGR over FY25–FY30, supported by (a) a housing shortage of ~30 Mn units (2012 MOHUA estimate, still relevant), (b) the formalisation of self-employed borrowers who previously relied on informal lenders, and (c) PMAY 2.0 policy support. A 22% CAGR applied to a ₹10 lakh crore base yields a ₹27 lakh crore opportunity by FY30 — i.e., the addressable pool more than doubles in five years, providing ample room for 25%+ AUM growers like HOMEFIRST.

**Policy Tailwinds.**
(a) **PMAY 2.0** — The Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana has been renewed with an enhanced subsidy framework; HOMEFIRST has already onboarded 38,507 beneficiaries with ₹966.4 Cr of subsidy credit flowing through (a ~26% loan reduction for the borrower)[^ip-latest]. This both subsidises demand and creates a regulatory-favoured channel partner status; (b) **RBI Priority Sector Lending (PSL)** — housing loans up to ₹35 lakh qualify for PSL status, giving banks an incentive to source loans through co-lending partnerships with HFCs like HOMEFIRST; (c) **LCR / NHB Refinance** — NHB remains a 17% lender to the company and provides a quasi-sovereign funding backstop; (d) **Green Finance** — IFC ($75 Mn women-borrower line) and DFC ($75 Mn green-housing line) provide concessional foreign-currency funding that supports both ESG ratings and cost of borrowing; (e) **RBI's Expected Credit Loss (ECL) framework under Ind AS 109** — the company's Stage-3 PCR (provision coverage ratio) of 44.9% is conservative vs peers, providing a cushion in any asset-quality downturn.[^ip-latest][^ar-fy25]

**Promoter Pedigree & Tenure.**
The "promoter" construct in HOMEFIRST is unconventional but governance-friendly. The two largest historical sponsors — **True North Fund V LLP** (a leading Indian mid-market private-equity firm, founded by Vishal Nevatia) and **Aether (Mauritius) Limited** — together hold just ~7% as of Jun 2026, down from 30.19% in Sep 2023[^ar-fy25][^cc-transcript]. The continuing stake decline reflects orderly monetisation rather than loss of confidence — bulk deals show True North and Aether selling ₹334 Cr and ₹326 Cr respectively on Feb 26, 2026 at ₹1,190, with the paper being picked up by long-only institutional investors (Pictet, Societe Generale, BNP Paribas), suggesting a clean transition from PE-style holders to long-term holders[^cc-transcript]. **Manoj Viswanathan (MD & CEO)** has been with the company for over a decade and brings continuity — a critical factor in an HFC where underwriting culture takes 3–5 years to build. Total director remuneration is ₹1.76 Cr (0.09% of revenue), well within the 1% cap, and MD/CEO compensation at ₹3.66 Cr (including a one-time bonus of ₹1.92 Cr) is reasonable for the scale and complexity of the franchise.[^ar-fy25]

**FCF Deployment Track Record (3 Years).**
HFC cash flows are structurally distorted because the company is in a growth phase — it lends out more than it collects each year, so CFO (cash flow from operations) is negative even as PAT is positive. FY26 is illustrative: PAT of ₹540 Cr but CFO of -₹1,940 Cr, driven by a ₹24,839 Cr increase in the loan book[^ar-fy25]. **This is normal for an HFC** — think of it like a manufacturer building inventory. The right question is: is the growth capital being deployed at attractive returns? On this metric, HOMEFIRST scores well: incremental spread is 5.40% vs stock spread of 5.3% (meaning every fresh loan is more profitable than the legacy book); incremental ROA is well above the stock ROA of 3.9%; and credit cost on incremental book has been stable at ~40 bps[^ip-latest]. So capital is being deployed at improving, not deteriorating, unit economics. **Three specific deployment choices stand out as quality capital allocation**: (a) the FY26 ₹1,250 Cr QIP that lifted CRAR from 32.8% to 44.1% and reduced leverage from 3.8x to 2.4x — a clear deleveraging decision that improved optionality; (b) origination investment in branch + tech (61 physical + 137 digital branches in pipeline); and (c) Q1 FY27 NCD allotment (Aug 28, 2026, separate from the ₹150 Cr proposed) which diversifies the funding mix further.[^ar-fy25][^ip-latest]

**Debt Management & Credit Ratings.**
Leverage is 3.5x (down from 3.8x in FY25), CRAR is 42.6% (vs the RBI-mandated minimum of 15% for HFCs) — this gives the company 2–3 years of growth runway without any further equity raise. Cost of borrowing has been managed down from 8.4% (FY24) to 8.1% (FY26) to 7.8% (Q1 FY27), a 60 bps compression that flows directly into NIM expansion[^ip-latest]. The funding mix is highly diversified across 32 lenders: private banks 35%, PSU banks 22%, NHB 17%, NCDs 14%, ECBs 5%, Direct Assignment 4%, co-lending 2%, NBFCs 1% — and critically, **zero commercial paper exposure**[^ip-latest]. Credit ratings are **AA (Stable)** from ICRA, CARE, and India Ratings — all three agencies, confirming institutional-grade credit quality. There is meaningful headroom for a rating upgrade to AA+, which could lower incremental cost of borrowing by 10–20 bps.

**ANALYTICAL COMMENTARY — CASH FLOW & KEY RATIOS**  
Negative CFO is structural for HFCs in growth phase: every ₹100 Cr of AUM growth requires ~₹85 Cr of incremental borrowings (at 3.5x leverage), but the cash outflow for loan disbursement (₹16,284 Cr in Q1FY27 annualized) far exceeds operating profit (PPOP ₹2,235 Cr quarterly). Thus CFO ≈ PAT – Net Loan Growth. FY26A CFO improved to -₹1,940 Cr from -₹2,196 Cr (FY25A) as disbursement growth moderated slightly and collections held up (98.4% efficiency). We project CFO turning more negative in FY27E–FY28E (-₹3,230 Cr, -₹4,160 Cr) as 25% AUM growth accelerates on a larger base. This is not a distress signal — it is the "funding gap" filled by CFF (fresh borrowings + DA proceeds). Free Cash Flow (CFO – Capex) mirrors CFO as capex is tiny (₹10–20 Cr/yr, tech-only). CFO/EBITDA is deeply negative but meaningless for lenders; the correct metric is **Incremental Credit Cost / Incremental AUM** (40 bps) and **Liquidity Coverage** (12% buffer). ROCE (asset-side, ~10%) is stable because spread income scales with assets while equity base expands via retained earnings + QIP. ROE dipped to 12.4% in FY26A due to QIP dilution (equity base +50% YoY) but recovers to 13.4%/14.8% as PAT growth outpaces equity growth. Net D/E rises from 2.43x to 3.0x by FY28E — still conservative vs 5–6x peers (Aavas, Aadhar) and well within the 7x regulatory cap. Debtor days are irrelevant (loan repayments via e-NACH/auto-debit); inventory/payables N/A for financial services.
